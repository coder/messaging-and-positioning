# Engineering Manager

## Role Summary

Engineering Managers lead one or more software engineering teams and are accountable for what those teams deliver, how well they deliver it, and how effectively the engineers on them work and grow. They translate product and business priorities into plans their teams can execute, remove obstacles that slow delivery, and balance feature work against reliability, technical debt, and operational responsibilities. Engineering Managers sit between individual developers and executive engineering leadership. They feel the day-to-day cost of slow onboarding, inconsistent environments, and tooling friction through their team's output, and they are often the first leader to sponsor a new tool or workflow, including AI coding agents, for their team.

## Related Titles

- Engineering Manager
- Senior Engineering Manager
- Software Development Manager
- Software Engineering Manager
- Director of Engineering
- Senior Director of Engineering

Organizations structure engineering management differently. These titles share accountability for one or more delivery teams, the people on them, and the outcomes they ship, although scope ranges from a single team to a group of teams. Vice President of Engineering, Head of Engineering, and other executive engineering titles are covered on the [CTO](./cto.md) page, since they own organization-wide engineering strategy and budget rather than the delivery of specific teams. Individual contributors, including tech leads and staff engineers without people-management responsibility, are covered on the [Developer](./developer.md) page.

## Primary Goals

- Deliver committed work predictably while maintaining quality, reliability, and security.
- Increase the team's throughput by removing bottlenecks in environments, tooling, reviews, and handoffs.
- Get new hires and engineers moving between projects productive as quickly as possible.
- Keep engineers focused on building software rather than configuring tools or waiting on infrastructure.
- Help the team adopt AI coding assistants and agents in ways that measurably improve delivery without lowering code quality.
- Balance new feature work with technical debt, bug fixes, and operational responsibilities.
- Build a healthy, engaged team and retain strong engineers.
- Make the case to leadership for the headcount, tools, and investments the team needs.

## Core Responsibilities

- Plan and prioritize the team's work with product management and other stakeholders.
- Track delivery progress, identify risks early, and adjust plans when priorities change.
- Hire, onboard, coach, and develop engineers, and manage performance.
- Identify and escalate the obstacles that slow the team down, from flaky environments to approval bottlenecks.
- Participate in technical design and architecture discussions, and ensure decisions are made at the right level.
- Decide which tools and workflows the team adopts, within the standards set by platform, security, and engineering leadership.
- Manage the team's budget for tools and services where they hold one, and justify investments to leadership.
- Represent the team's needs to platform engineering, developer experience, security, and IT.
- Own the team's operational responsibilities, including on-call rotations and incident follow-up.

## Daily Workflow

An Engineering Manager's day is mostly made up of conversations: standups, one-on-ones, planning and prioritization with product, design reviews, and cross-team coordination. In between, they review delivery progress, unblock engineers, respond to escalations, and work through hiring and onboarding.

Much of their attention goes to what is slowing the team down. A new hire who can't get a working environment for a week, an engineer waiting on access to a service, a build that only works on one laptop, or an AI tool that security hasn't approved all show up first as missed estimates and frustrated engineers on their team.

As AI coding assistants and agents become part of everyday development, Engineering Managers are increasingly responsible for deciding how their team uses them, setting expectations for reviewing AI-generated code, and showing whether these tools actually change delivery outcomes.

## Success Metrics

- Delivery against commitments and roadmap predictability.
- Team throughput, cycle time, and lead time for changes.
- Software quality, including escaped defects and production incidents.
- Time for new hires and engineers changing projects to reach their first meaningful contribution.
- Team engagement, satisfaction, and retention.
- Time engineers spend on product work versus environment setup, waiting, and toil.
- Measurable impact of AI-assisted development on the team's delivery.
- Hiring and development of engineers on the team.

## What Matters Most

### Predictable delivery

Engineering Managers are judged on whether their team ships what it committed to. Anything that makes delivery less predictable, such as environment drift, slow builds, or blocked access, directly affects how leadership sees the team.

### Getting engineers productive fast

Every week a new hire or reassigned engineer spends setting up an environment is a week of lost capacity. Engineering Managers want engineers contributing on their first day on a team or project, not after a long setup and approval process.

### Removing friction for the team

Engineering Managers see the cumulative cost of small delays across every engineer on the team. They value tools and platforms that remove waiting, setup, and troubleshooting so the team can stay focused on building software.

### Adopting AI without losing control of quality

Engineering Managers are under pressure to show productivity gains from AI coding tools, while remaining accountable for the quality and security of what the team ships. They want AI tools their engineers will actually use, that the organization has approved, and that fit the team's existing review and delivery practices.

### A team that wants to stay

Modern tooling and efficient workflows affect retention. Engineers who spend their days fighting environments and waiting on tickets are more likely to leave, and Engineering Managers bear the cost of replacing them.

## Biggest Challenges

### Onboarding and project ramp-up

Getting a new engineer, contractor, or reassigned teammate a working environment often depends on tribal knowledge, manual setup, and approvals outside the team's control, delaying their first contribution by days or weeks.

### Hidden productivity loss

Time lost to environment issues, slow feedback loops, and waiting on infrastructure rarely shows up in planning tools, which makes it hard to quantify and harder to get prioritized.

### Proving the value of AI tools

Leadership expects AI coding tools to improve delivery, but adoption is uneven and results are hard to measure. Engineering Managers must show real impact while ensuring AI-generated code meets the team's standards.

### Dependence on other teams

Many of the obstacles slowing their team, such as environment provisioning, access requests, security approvals, and tool rollouts, are owned by platform, security, or IT teams with their own priorities.

### Balancing delivery with long-term health

Roadmap pressure competes with time for technical debt, tooling improvements, and engineer development. Engineering Managers must make that tradeoff continually and defend it to stakeholders.

## Common Frustrations

- New hires and reassigned engineers take too long to make their first contribution.
- Estimates slip because of environment issues and "works on my machine" problems.
- Engineers wait on tickets for access, infrastructure, or approvals.
- Developers adopt AI tools inconsistently, or use unapproved tools because approved options lag behind.
- Productivity losses from tooling friction are hard to quantify for leadership.
- Fixing the team's tooling problems depends on other teams' roadmaps.
- Mandated tools that slow engineers down become the manager's problem to manage.

## Collaboration

Engineering Managers work most closely with the developers on their teams and with product managers and designers on what to build. They depend on platform engineering for the environments and infrastructure their teams use, on developer experience teams to identify and address friction across the engineering organization, and on security and IT for access and approvals. They report to directors, VPs of Engineering, or the CTO, and translate team-level needs into the business terms those leaders use to prioritize investment.

## What They Need from Platform and Developer Experience Teams

Engineering Managers rely on [Platform Engineers](./platform-engineer.md) and [Developer Experience Leads](./developer-experience-lead.md) to provide the foundation their teams build on. They value:

- Ready-to-use development environments that let engineers contribute on their first day.
- Self-service access to the infrastructure, services, and tools their team needs, without tickets for routine work.
- Consistent environments that eliminate setup differences between engineers.
- Approved, well-supported access to AI coding assistants and agents.
- Visibility into how their team uses development environments and AI tools.
- A clear way to raise friction their team is experiencing and see it addressed.

## Buying Influence

Engineering Managers are frequently the first internal sponsor for developer infrastructure. They surface the problem, often in terms of onboarding time, delivery delays, or developer frustration, and they bring teams willing to run a pilot. Directors of Engineering may hold budget for tools used across their teams, but platform-wide investments usually require approval from a VP of Engineering, CTO, or CIO. Engineering Managers influence that decision through pilot results, team feedback, and the business case they help build, and their teams' adoption after purchase determines whether the investment succeeds.

## Common Questions

When evaluating a platform like Coder, Engineering Managers often ask:

- How quickly can a new engineer on my team get a working environment?
- Will my engineers find this faster than their current setup, or will they route around it?
- Can my team keep using its preferred IDEs, tools, and AI coding assistants?
- How much effort will migrating my team require, and will it disrupt delivery?
- Who on the platform team owns templates and support, and how do we request changes?
- Can my team use AI coding agents in a way security will approve?
- How will we know whether it improved our team's delivery?
- What does a pilot with my team look like?

## Content They Trust

- Peer recommendations and experiences from other engineering managers.
- Customer stories that show measurable improvements in onboarding time and delivery.
- Hands-on pilots with their own team.
- Feedback from their own engineers.
- Engineering blogs and conference talks about team productivity and developer experience.
- Product documentation and demos that show real developer workflows.
