# Platform Engineer

## Role Summary

Platform Engineers design, build, and operate the internal platforms that developers rely on to build, test, and deploy software. They create standardized, self-service infrastructure, development environments, and workflows that improve developer productivity while maintaining security, reliability, and operational consistency. They balance the needs of developers with the requirements of security, infrastructure, and operations, building platforms that scale across the organization and enable teams to adopt cloud-native technologies and AI-assisted software development with confidence.

Organizations structure platform engineering differently. Depending on the company, these responsibilities may sit within Platform Engineering, Developer Experience, Infrastructure Engineering, Cloud Engineering, or Site Reliability Engineering. While titles vary, the role is responsible for building and operating the internal platforms developers rely on every day.

## Primary Goals

- Increase developer productivity by providing fast, reliable, and self-service development platforms.
- Standardize development environments to improve consistency, security, and maintainability.
- Reduce operational toil through automation, infrastructure as code, and platform self-service.
- Build scalable platforms that support growing engineering teams and increasingly complex workloads.
- Improve the reliability, availability, and performance of developer infrastructure.
- Enable developers to adopt new technologies, including AI-assisted software development, without increasing operational risk.
- Balance developer flexibility with organizational requirements for security, governance, and compliance.
- Optimize infrastructure utilization and operational costs without sacrificing the developer experience.
- Integrate development platforms with the organization's existing cloud, identity, security, and engineering toolchains.

## Core Responsibilities

- Design, build, and operate the internal developer platform used across the organization.
- Manage development environments, infrastructure, and platform services that support software development.
- Standardize developer workflows through reusable templates, automation, and infrastructure as code.
- Integrate developer platforms with cloud infrastructure, identity providers, source control, CI/CD, security tools, and engineering systems.
- Maintain the reliability, scalability, security, and performance of developer infrastructure and platform services.
- Enable self-service access to development resources while enforcing organizational policies and governance.
- Partner with engineering teams to improve the developer experience and remove friction throughout the software development lifecycle.
- Collaborate with security, infrastructure, and IT teams to ensure development platforms meet organizational security, compliance, and operational requirements.
- Evaluate, implement, and operationalize new developer technologies, including AI-assisted software development and developer tooling.
- Continuously measure platform performance, developer experience, adoption, and operational efficiency to drive ongoing improvements.

## Daily Workflow

Platform Engineers split their time between building and operating the internal developer platform, supporting engineering teams, and continuously improving the developer experience. Like product managers, they treat internal developers as their customers, gathering feedback, identifying friction, and prioritizing platform improvements that increase productivity across the organization.

A typical day may involve developing infrastructure as code, maintaining platform services, troubleshooting developer issues, reviewing infrastructure changes, or automating manual processes. They regularly collaborate with software engineers, security, infrastructure, and IT teams to evaluate new technologies, implement platform standards, and integrate developer tooling.

As organizations adopt AI-assisted software development, Platform Engineers are increasingly responsible for operationalizing new tools and ensuring they can be deployed, managed, and governed at scale. Throughout their work, they monitor platform health, platform adoption, and developer feedback to improve reliability, reduce operational overhead, and continuously evolve tooling to meet the changing needs of their users.

## What Matters Most

### Empowering developers through self-service

Platform Engineers want developers to spend their time creating software, not managing infrastructure. They prioritize self-service platforms, standardized workflows, and automation that remove friction without sacrificing flexibility.

### Building reliable, scalable platforms

The internal developer platform is critical infrastructure. Platform Engineers value reliability, scalability, and operational simplicity, ensuring the platform can support growing engineering organizations with minimal operational overhead.

### Automating repetitive work

Manual processes do not scale. Platform Engineers continuously replace manual provisioning, configuration, and maintenance with automation and infrastructure as code, allowing small teams to support large developer populations efficiently.

### Standardization without sacrificing flexibility

Developers need consistent environments that are secure, maintainable, and easy to support, but they also need the flexibility to choose the tools and workflows that make them productive. Platform Engineers strive to balance both.

### Enabling AI adoption responsibly

As AI becomes part of everyday software development, Platform Engineers want to make new tools available quickly while ensuring they can be deployed, managed, and integrated into existing platforms without creating unnecessary operational complexity or security risk.

### Optimizing AI infrastructure and costs

As AI becomes a core part of software development, Platform Engineers need visibility into how developers and AI agents consume models, compute, and infrastructure. They prioritize understanding AI cost drivers, optimizing resource utilization, and ensuring organizations can scale AI adoption without allowing infrastructure or model costs to grow unchecked.

### Observability and operational insight

Platform Engineers need visibility into how developers and AI tools use the platform. They value comprehensive telemetry, auditing, and usage data that help them troubleshoot issues, optimize infrastructure, measure adoption, and make informed decisions about platform improvements.

## Biggest Challenges

### Balancing standardization with developer flexibility

Developers want the freedom to choose the tools, workflows, and environments that make them productive, while organizations need consistency, security, and operational simplicity. Platform Engineers must build platforms that provide both.

### Keeping pace with AI innovation

New AI models, coding assistants, and autonomous agents are emerging faster than most organizations can evaluate and operationalize them. Platform Engineers must enable developers to adopt these tools quickly while ensuring they integrate cleanly with existing platforms, infrastructure, and security requirements.

### Managing AI adoption sustainably

As developers increasingly rely on AI-powered coding tools and autonomous agents, Platform Engineers must balance rapid adoption with operational efficiency. They need visibility into AI usage, model consumption, infrastructure demand, and associated costs so they can scale AI across the organization without creating operational bottlenecks or uncontrolled spending.

### Building platforms that developers actually adopt

An internal platform only creates value if developers choose to use it. Platform Engineers must continuously gather feedback, remove friction, and improve the developer experience while balancing organizational requirements.

### Scaling platform operations without scaling headcount

Engineering organizations continue to grow while platform teams often remain relatively small. Platform Engineers must automate provisioning, maintenance, upgrades, and operational workflows so they can support more developers without proportionally increasing operational overhead.

### Maintaining visibility across increasingly complex platforms

Modern developer platforms span cloud providers, Kubernetes, identity systems, AI models, development tools, and security controls. Maintaining end-to-end observability and quickly identifying issues across this growing ecosystem is increasingly difficult.

## Common Frustrations

- Developers bypass the platform because it's slower or less flexible than local development.
- Supporting a growing number of developer tools, AI capabilities, and workflows with limited time and resources.
- Manual operational work that should be automated through infrastructure as code and self-service.
- Balancing developer flexibility with organizational requirements for security, governance, and standardization.
- Limited visibility into platform usage, AI adoption, and the operational costs they create.
- Growing platform complexity as cloud infrastructure, AI tools, identity, and security systems become more interconnected.
- Supporting a growing engineering organization without proportionally increasing platform team headcount.

## Collaboration

Platform Engineers build platforms that serve other engineers. A small platform team is often responsible for the daily experience of hundreds or even thousands of developers, making them one of the highest-leverage teams in an engineering organization. To deliver that experience, they collaborate closely with software developers to understand pain points and improve workflows, partner with infrastructure and cloud teams to manage the underlying platform, work with security and IT teams to implement governance and identity controls, and align with engineering leadership on platform priorities and technology adoption. Success depends on balancing the needs of many stakeholders while delivering secure, reliable, and self-service platforms that scale across the organization.

## What They Need from End Users

Platform Engineers rely on developers and other end users to be active partners in building a better internal platform. The most successful platforms evolve through continuous feedback, shared ownership, and widespread adoption.

- Adopt standardized tools, templates, and workflows where they improve consistency and productivity.
- Provide honest, actionable feedback about friction points and opportunities for improvement.
- Embrace self-service instead of relying on manual support whenever possible.
- Share evolving requirements as new technologies, frameworks, and AI tools become part of everyday development.
- Look for repeatable solutions that can be standardized and automated rather than requesting one-off exceptions.
- Partner with the platform team to improve the developer experience across the entire engineering organization, not just for individual teams.

## Buying Influence

Platform Engineers play a critical role in the technical evaluation, implementation, and long-term success of developer infrastructure platforms. As organizations move from identifying a need to evaluating solutions, Platform Engineers are responsible for validating technical fit, integrating the platform with existing infrastructure, and ensuring it can be operated securely and reliably at scale. They work closely with Developer Experience teams, engineering leadership, and developers throughout the evaluation process.

While Platform Engineers assess operational and technical requirements, they also look for evidence that a solution delivers a better developer experience and will be broadly adopted. Their recommendation carries significant weight because they are ultimately responsible for operating the platform long after it's deployed.

## Common Questions

When evaluating a platform like Coder, Platform Engineers often ask:

- How does Coder integrate with our existing cloud, Kubernetes, identity, networking, and security infrastructure?
- How do we deploy, configure, and manage Coder using infrastructure as code?
- How much operational overhead will this add for our platform team?
- How customizable are workspace templates, provisioning workflows, and developer environments?
- Can developers continue using their preferred IDEs, tools, and AI coding assistants?
- How do we migrate developers to Coder with minimal disruption to their existing workflows?
- What visibility do we have into workspace usage, platform health, resource utilization, and AI activity?
- How does Coder support our security, compliance, and governance requirements?
- How does Coder scale as our engineering organization grows?
- How can we validate that developers will adopt Coder before rolling it out across the organization?
