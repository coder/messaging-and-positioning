# Chief Information Security Officer (CISO)

## Role Summary

The Chief Information Security Officer (CISO) is responsible for protecting the organization's information, systems, and technology while enabling the business to operate securely. They lead security strategy, manage cyber risk, oversee governance and compliance, and ensure security is integrated across technology, engineering, and business operations. Modern CISOs work closely with engineering, IT, legal, and executive leadership to help the organization adopt new technologies, including AI, without introducing unnecessary risk. Unlike the CIO, who leads enterprise technology, or the CTO, who leads engineering strategy and software development, the CISO is primarily accountable for strengthening the organization's security posture and managing cyber risk.

## Related Titles

- Chief Security Officer
- Vice President of Information Security
- Vice President of Cybersecurity
- Head of Information Security
- Head of Cybersecurity
- Director of Information Security
- Director of Cybersecurity

The CISO's responsibilities may be shared across several security leaders depending on the size and maturity of the organization. These titles share responsibility for defining security strategy, managing cyber risk, overseeing governance and compliance, and protecting the organization's information systems and technology assets. The CIO and CTO are not treated as related titles in this persona. They are distinct executive roles with overlapping interests but different primary responsibilities.

## Primary Goals

- Protect the organization from cyber threats while enabling the business to operate securely.
- Reduce organizational cyber risk without unnecessarily slowing innovation or software delivery.
- Strengthen the organization's overall security posture across people, processes, and technology.
- Build security into software development, cloud infrastructure, and enterprise technology from the start.
- Maintain compliance with regulatory, industry, and customer security requirements.
- Improve organizational resilience through effective incident preparedness, response, and recovery.
- Enable the secure adoption of emerging technologies, including AI and cloud-native development.
- Increase visibility into security risks across users, applications, infrastructure, and data.
- Foster a security-first culture through collaboration, education, and shared accountability.
- Build trust with customers, partners, regulators, and executive leadership through strong security governance.

## Core Responsibilities

- Define and execute the organization's information security and cybersecurity strategy.
- Lead the teams responsible for cybersecurity, security operations, identity and access management, governance, risk, and compliance.
- Establish security policies, standards, controls, and governance frameworks across the organization.
- Oversee threat detection, vulnerability management, incident response, and business continuity planning.
- Protect the organization's applications, infrastructure, data, and software supply chain from security threats.
- Partner with engineering and platform teams to integrate security into software development and infrastructure.
- Collaborate with the CIO to secure enterprise technology and IT operations, and with the CTO to embed security into engineering platforms and development workflows.
- Manage security risk assessments, regulatory compliance, third-party risk, and customer security requirements.
- Guide the secure evaluation and adoption of emerging technologies, including AI, cloud services, and developer tools.
- Represent the organization's security strategy to executive leadership, customers, regulators, auditors, and strategic partners.

## Daily Workflow

The CISO operates at the intersection of security, technology, and business strategy. Their day is split between overseeing the organization's security posture, advising leadership on emerging risks, and working with engineering and IT teams to ensure security is built into new initiatives rather than added after the fact.

Priorities shift constantly. A typical day might include reviewing a critical vulnerability, preparing for a customer security assessment, evaluating the security implications of adopting a new AI tool, meeting with engineering teams to improve secure development practices, or briefing executives on the organization's evolving risk landscape.

Success depends on making informed, risk-based decisions rather than eliminating risk entirely. The CISO must weigh security controls against their impact on developer productivity, employee experience, operational efficiency, and business objectives, helping the organization move quickly while maintaining an appropriate level of protection.

## Success Metrics

CISOs are evaluated by how effectively they protect the organization from threats while enabling the business to operate securely, maintain customer trust, and manage risk.

- Overall organizational security posture and risk reduction.
- Ability to prevent, detect, and respond to security incidents.
- Recovery time and organizational resilience following security events.
- Compliance with regulatory, industry, and customer security requirements.
- Adoption and effectiveness of security policies, controls, and governance programs.
- Reduction of security risk across applications, infrastructure, identities, and data.
- Integration of security into software development and technology operations.
- Secure adoption of emerging technologies, including AI and cloud services.
- Customer, auditor, and executive confidence in the organization's security program.
- Security team effectiveness, talent development, and operational maturity.
- Business continuity and organizational resilience.
- Security investments that measurably reduce risk while supporting business objectives.

## What Matters Most

### Reducing organizational risk

CISOs focus on reducing the likelihood and impact of security threats across the organization. They prioritize the risks that could disrupt operations, expose sensitive data, damage customer trust, or create financial and legal consequences.

### Enabling the business securely

Security should enable the business, not unnecessarily slow it down. CISOs look for ways to embed security into technology and development workflows so employees and developers can move quickly within well-defined guardrails.

### Maintaining visibility, governance, and control

CISOs need confidence that security policies are consistently enforced across users, applications, infrastructure, and data. Centralized visibility, auditability, and governance allow them to detect risk, investigate incidents, demonstrate compliance, and make informed decisions.

### Building organizational resilience

Security incidents are inevitable, making resilience just as important as prevention. CISOs prioritize incident response, business continuity, and recovery capabilities that minimize disruption and allow the organization to recover quickly.

### Securely adopting new technologies

Whether adopting cloud platforms, AI, or new developer tools, CISOs are responsible for balancing innovation with security. They want to help the organization embrace new technologies while maintaining appropriate controls over identities, data, infrastructure, and third-party services.

## Biggest Challenges

### Managing the rapid evolution of AI risk

AI is changing both sides of cybersecurity. Organizations are adopting AI-powered developer tools and business applications at the same time attackers are using AI to identify vulnerabilities, automate attacks, and increase the speed and sophistication of cyber threats. CISOs must continuously evaluate new risks while helping the business adopt AI responsibly.

### Balancing security with business velocity

Security teams are expected to reduce risk without slowing software delivery, limiting developer productivity, or creating unnecessary friction for employees and the business.

### Securing increasingly complex technology environments

Cloud infrastructure, SaaS applications, AI services, third-party software, and distributed workforces have significantly expanded the attack surface. Maintaining consistent visibility, governance, and security controls across these environments continues to grow more difficult.

### Demonstrating measurable security outcomes

Security investments are expected to produce clear business value, but reducing risk is difficult to quantify. CISOs must communicate security effectiveness to executives and boards using meaningful business outcomes rather than technical activity alone.

### Building security into technology adoption

Security teams are often brought into technology decisions too late. CISOs must establish governance that enables engineering, IT, and business teams to adopt new technologies securely from the outset instead of relying on security as a final checkpoint.

## Common Frustrations

- Security is brought into technology decisions after key architecture and purchasing decisions have already been made.
- Employees without software engineering expertise can now build and deploy code using AI, expanding the organization's attack surface.
- Security controls are perceived as obstacles rather than enablers of the business.
- Developers and business teams bypass approved tools and security policies to move faster.
- Shadow AI, SaaS applications, and unmanaged developer tools create visibility and governance gaps.
- The pace of AI-powered threats and newly discovered vulnerabilities is outstripping traditional security practices.
- Executives expect clear evidence that security investments are reducing business risk.
- Security teams are expected to protect a growing attack surface with limited budget and headcount.
- Regulatory, customer, and audit requirements consume time that could otherwise be spent improving the organization's overall security posture.

## Collaboration

The CISO works across nearly every function in the organization to embed security into technology, business processes, and day-to-day operations. Success depends on building strong partnerships rather than acting as an isolated gatekeeper. They work closely with engineering, platform engineering, IT, and infrastructure teams to secure software development, cloud platforms, identities, endpoints, and enterprise systems. They also partner with legal, compliance, privacy, procurement, and risk teams to meet regulatory obligations, manage third-party risk, and respond to customer security requirements. At the executive level, the CISO collaborates with the CIO on enterprise technology, the CTO on secure software development and engineering platforms, and business leaders to balance security, innovation, and operational objectives. They also regularly engage with customers, auditors, regulators, and executive leadership to communicate the organization's security posture and build confidence in its ability to manage risk.

## What They Need from Technology and Platform Leaders

CISOs rely on technology and platform leaders to build security into systems by design rather than treating it as a separate function. They want security controls that are consistent, scalable, and easy for developers and employees to adopt without creating unnecessary friction. They expect platform teams to standardize infrastructure, reduce unnecessary complexity, automate security controls wherever possible, and provide centralized visibility into how technology is being used. As AI adoption accelerates, they also need confidence that new developer tools, AI agents, and AI models can be deployed with appropriate governance, access controls, auditability, and policy enforcement. Most importantly, CISOs need partners who view security as a shared responsibility. The strongest technology organizations balance developer productivity with risk management, enabling the business to move quickly while maintaining strong security and compliance.

## Buying Influence

CISOs are executive sponsors and key technical stakeholders for security, governance, and risk management initiatives. While they may not always own the budget for developer platforms or engineering tools, they are often responsible for evaluating whether new technologies meet the organization's security, compliance, and governance requirements. They influence purchasing decisions by assessing how solutions reduce organizational risk, improve visibility and auditability, protect sensitive data and intellectual property, and support secure adoption of emerging technologies, including AI. Their approval is often essential for technologies that impact software development, cloud infrastructure, identity, or access to sensitive systems.

## Common Questions

- How do we reduce organizational risk without slowing the business?
- How can we enable developers to use AI without losing visibility or control?
- How do we govern AI models, AI agents, and AI-generated code across the organization?
- How do we protect source code and sensitive data as software development becomes increasingly AI-driven?
- How do we reduce the organization's attack surface without limiting developer productivity?
- How can we enforce consistent security policies across developers, AI agents, and development environments?
- How do we demonstrate the value and effectiveness of our security program to executives and the board?
- How can we give developers the tools they want while maintaining compliance and security requirements?
- How do we secure software development as more employees gain access to AI-powered coding tools?
- How do we build security into our engineering platforms instead of relying on manual processes and individual developers?

## Content They Trust

CISOs rely on credible, evidence-based information to evaluate technologies that reduce risk and strengthen the organization's security posture. They prioritize independent validation, technical depth, customer proof, and guidance from trusted security practitioners over marketing claims.

- Security architecture documentation and technical implementation guides.
- Compliance certifications, third-party audits, and security attestations.
- Customer case studies from organizations with similar security and regulatory requirements.
- Peer recommendations from security leaders and practitioner communities.
- Independent security research, threat intelligence, and analyst reports.
- Industry conferences, security briefings, and practitioner-led presentations.
